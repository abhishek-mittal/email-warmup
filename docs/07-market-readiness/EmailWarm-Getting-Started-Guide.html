PART 1

What EmailWarm does

Email providers like Gmail, Outlook and Yahoo are cautious about new or quiet email addresses. If an address that normally sends nothing suddenly sends lots of mail, they assume it might be spam and hide it in the spam folder. That means the people you write to never see your messages.

EmailWarm fixes this by building trust slowly. It sends a small number of friendly, harmless emails each day from your address to a set of helper mailboxes. Those helpers open the emails, reply to them, and — if an email lands in spam — move it back to the inbox. Each day it sends a few more. After a few weeks, the providers have learned that your address is a normal, trusted sender, and your real mail starts landing in the inbox.

Your mailboxsends → Helper mailboxopens · replies · rescues from spam → Trust goes up

The three kinds of mailbox in this guide

The mailbox you want to warm

The address whose reputation you want to build — for example your personal Gmail or your work address. The app calls this an inbox.

Helper mailboxes

Other real addresses that trade warm-up emails with it. They are never warmed themselves; they just help. The app calls these pool inboxes. You need at least two.

Judge mailboxes

Used only for a placement test (Part 10) to see where your mail lands. The app calls these seeds. Optional until you reach that part.

Good to know

This is your own private setup on one computer. Everything stays with you; no outside companies are involved in the warming.

Please read before connecting anything Warming sends real emails from whatever address you connect, and once an email is sent it cannot be called back. For your first try, use an address you are comfortable experimenting with — a spare or personal one — rather than your most important business mailbox. Most importantly, your helper mailboxes must be on a different email domain than the mailbox you are warming. (The domain is the part after the @ sign.) For example, warm you@yourcompany.com and use helpers at @gmail.com and @outlook.com. The app will refuse to pair two addresses that share the same domain.

1One mailbox you want to warm

A real email address you can log in to. A personal Gmail or Outlook address is perfect for a first run. Note its domain (the part after @).

2Two or three helper mailboxes on other domains

For example, if you are warming a @gmail.com address, your helpers could be an @outlook.com address and a @yahoo.com address. A mix of providers works best. You'll need to log in to each helper to see the emails arrive.

3Three more mailboxes as "judges" only for Part 10

Needed only when you run a placement test. You can add these later.

4The EmailWarm app, running on a computer

If a colleague has set this up for you, you only need the web address (usually http://localhost:3000). If not, Part 4 shows the three short commands to start it, or you can ask whoever set up the project to start it for you.

5Two-step verification switched on for each mailbox

Gmail and Yahoo only let you create the special "app password" (Part 5) when two-step verification is already on. Turn it on now in each account's security settings — it takes two minutes per account.

PART 3

Which email addresses you can use

You can warm almost any real mailbox. Here is exactly what works and what to expect for each.

Personal Gmail (name@gmail.com)

Fully supported, and the easiest to start with. You will turn on two-step verification, create an app password, and make sure IMAP is switched on (that's the setting that lets the app read the mailbox). All three are quick and are covered in Part 5.

Personal Outlook / Hotmail / Live (name@outlook.com, @hotmail.com, @live.com)

Supported. Personal Microsoft accounts can create an app password once two-step verification is on. A few older accounts hide this option; if yours does, use a Gmail or Yahoo address for your first run instead.

Yahoo (name@yahoo.com)

Supported, the same way as Gmail: two-step verification, then an app password.

Your own company domain (name@yourcompany.com)

Supported. How you get the settings depends on who runs your email:

  • Google Workspace (your company uses Gmail behind the scenes): treat it exactly like personal Gmail. Create an app password at your Google account. If the option is missing, your IT admin has turned it off — ask them to allow app passwords, or to enable it for your account.
  • Microsoft 365 (company Outlook): treat it like personal Outlook. Many company accounts block app passwords by policy; if so, ask IT, or use a different mailbox for testing.
  • Zoho, GoDaddy, Namecheap, cPanel, or another host: you'll need the mail server details (below). You usually find these in your host's help pages under a title like "email client setup", "IMAP settings", or "connect with Outlook". Your IT person or host's support can give them in a minute.
The one thing every mailbox needs EmailWarm needs to both send and read mail for the address. "Read" is done through a setting called IMAP. If IMAP is turned off, the app can send but cannot open, reply or file the warm-up mail — and the mailbox can't take part. For Gmail, turn IMAP on under Settings → See all settings → Forwarding and POP/IMAP. For most others it is already on.

2Start the app only if the page didn't load

This part needs a colleague who is comfortable with a computer terminal, or you can follow along. Open three terminal windows in the project folder and run one block in each:

Window 1 — the database
docker compose up -d
Window 2 — the engine (leave it open)
cd backend pnpm install pnpm db:migrate pnpm start:dev
Window 3 — the website (leave it open)
cd frontend pnpm install pnpm dev
What to expectAfter a minute or two, the website window says "Ready". Now http://localhost:3000/sign-in will open.
Clear the sample data first The app may come with pretend practice mailboxes. If you leave them in, they become your warm-up partners and the emails to them bounce, which pauses your real mailbox. Clear them once, in a terminal in the backend folder, with: pnpm db:seed:dev --reset. Your real mailboxes are never affected — it only removes practice addresses that end in .test.

3Sign in

On the sign-in page, use the ready-made test account:

Emailadmin@emailwarm.dev
PasswordEmailWarm-Phase1-Test!

PART 5

Get an "app password" for each mailbox

An app password is a one-off password that lets a program use your mailbox safely, without your real password. You create one on the email provider's own website, and you'll do this once for every address you connect — your mailbox to warm and each helper. Your normal password will not work.

1Gmail (and Google Workspace)

  1. Make sure two-step verification is on (Google account → Security).
  2. Go to myaccount.google.com/apppasswords.
  3. Type a name such as "EmailWarm" and click Create.
  4. Google shows a 16-letter code. Copy it now — you won't see it again. Spaces don't matter.
  5. Turn on IMAP: Gmail → Settings (gear) → See all settings → Forwarding and POP/IMAP → Enable IMAP → Save.
If the app-passwords page says it's unavailableTwo-step verification isn't on yet, or (for Workspace) your admin has disabled app passwords. Turn on two-step verification, or ask your admin — or use a personal Gmail for the first run.

2Outlook / Hotmail / Microsoft 365

  1. Sign in at account.microsoft.com → Security.
  2. Open two-step / advanced security options and turn on two-step verification.
  3. Find App passwords and create one named "EmailWarm". Copy the code.
If there is no "App passwords" optionSome personal and many company Microsoft accounts hide it. For your first run, use Gmail or Yahoo instead; you can add Outlook later.

3Yahoo

  1. Yahoo → Account Info → Account Security (two-step verification on).
  2. Click Generate app password, choose "Other app", name it "EmailWarm", copy the code.

4Your own company domain

If it's Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, follow step 1 or 2 above. For other hosts (Zoho, GoDaddy, cPanel, and so on), you typically use your normal mailbox password together with the server details from the Part 3 cheat sheet — unless the host offers app passwords, in which case use one. Your host's support or your IT person can confirm which.

Keep these safeTreat each app password like a key to that mailbox. Keep the codes somewhere private while you set up, and don't share them. You'll paste each one into the app in the next two parts.
PART 6

Add your helper mailboxes

Do this before connecting the mailbox you want to warm, so it has partners to practise with from its very first email.

1Open the Warming Pool page

In the left-hand menu, click Warming pool. It says "No pool inboxes yet." At the top right are two buttons: Add via wizard and Add via CSV.

2Add the first helper with the wizard

  1. Click Add via wizard.
  2. Set the type to Custom — even for a Gmail or Outlook helper. (The Gmail and Outlook options here ask for developer keys you don't have.)
  3. Fill in: the helper's email address; the outgoing (SMTP) server, port, user and password; and the incoming (IMAP) server, port, user and password. The "user" is the full email address. The "password" is the app password from Part 5, entered in both the SMTP and IMAP boxes. Use the Part 3 cheat sheet for the servers and ports.
  4. Save. Use Add another to add the next helper straight away.
What to expectThe helper appears in a grid, first as pending, then turning active within a minute once the app has checked its settings. A health score appears beside it.
If it says "Couldn't add this entry"Read the reason shown underneath. Usually it's a typo in the app password or a wrong port. "Duplicate email" means that address is already in the pool.

3Add two or three helpers in total, on different domains

More helpers means more realistic practice, and each helper only handles a limited amount per day. Make sure at least one helper uses a different provider from the others, and that none of them shares the domain of the mailbox you're about to warm.

What to expectAll helpers show active. Click one to open its page — it has tabs for Activity, Pairings and Logs, which stay empty until warm-up emails start flowing.
PART 7

Connect the mailbox you want to warm

1Open the connect page

In the left menu, click Accounts, then the button to connect a mailbox (or go to localhost:3000/inboxes/connect). You'll see three choices: Connect with Google, Connect with Microsoft, and Custom SMTP / IMAP.

Use the Custom optionThe Google and Microsoft buttons need sign-in keys that aren't set up on this computer yet. The Custom SMTP / IMAP card works with any mailbox — including Gmail and Outlook — using your app password.

2Fill in the Custom form

  1. Click the quick-fill button for your provider (Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo or Zoho). It fills in the servers and ports, and switches on IMAP for you.
  2. Type the email address, the SMTP user (the full address) and the SMTP password (your app password).
  3. Check the IMAP section is filled in — same user, same app password.
  4. Leave DKIM selector empty.
  5. Leave Join the shared warmup pool switched off for this first run. (It's for trading mail with other companies; you'll look at it in Part 12.)

3Click Connect and wait for the check

The app now logs in to the sending server, then the receiving server, then creates a folder called WarmupHub in your mailbox. This takes between 5 and 30 seconds.

What to expectA green result showing both checks passed, then the mailbox on the Accounts page marked active. Open the real mailbox in another browser tab — a new folder (a label in Gmail) called WarmupHub is now there. That proves the app can log in and file mail correctly.

If the check failsThe message tells you which step failed. "Wrong username or password" means the app password (check for typos; the username is the full address). A timeout usually means a wrong port or a network that blocks mail. Nothing is saved when a check fails, so just correct the field and try again. More help is in Part 13.
PART 8

Check the mailbox is healthy

Before warming, confirm the address is set up properly. These are behind-the-scenes records that prove an email genuinely comes from you.

1Open the mailbox and its DNS tab

On Accounts, click your mailbox. At the top you see its status, score and a trend arrow. Below are tabs: Activity Feed, DNS & Blacklist, Sent Emails, Placement and Logs. Open DNS & Blacklist and click Run DNS Check Now.

RecordWhat it means, simply
SPFA list of which servers are allowed to send mail for your address.
DKIMA hidden signature proving the email wasn't tampered with.
DMARCYour instructions for what to do with mail that fails the checks above.
MXWhere mail for your domain is delivered.
rDNSYour sending server points back to your domain. Minor, but helps.
What to expectGreen ticks for at least SPF, DKIM and MX. Gmail, Outlook and Yahoo addresses are set up by the provider, so they usually pass. "Unknown" means the app couldn't check — that's different from a failure.
If a record is red on your own company domainOpen the mailbox's diagnostics page for plain-English fix steps, add the record where you manage your domain's DNS (your registrar or host), and run the check again. DNS changes can take up to an hour to show.

2Run the blocklist check

On the same tab, click Run Blacklist Check Now. This asks several public "bad sender" lists whether your address or domain is on them.

What to expect"Clean". If something is listed, warm-up pauses on its own and the mailbox page explains why.
PART 9

Start warming & what to expect

Don't panic if nothing sends immediately — this is normal EmailWarm plans each day's emails once a day, at 10:30 in the morning (India time), and then spreads them out between about 1:30 pm and 11:30 pm, with random gaps and at least a few minutes between each one. The very first day it sends only a handful of emails (3 on the normal speed). So a freshly connected mailbox often shows no activity for a while. The next step lets you kick off today's batch right away.

1Start today's emails now

On the mailbox page, click Pause, then click Resume. Resuming schedules today's emails straight away (as long as the current time is before about 11:30 pm India time).

What to expectStatus returns to active. On the Activity Feed tab, upcoming emails appear and then turn into "sent" over the next minutes and hours.
If nothing gets scheduledCheck three things: the mailbox is active; you have at least one active helper on a different domain; and it's before late evening. With no eligible partner, the app sends nothing on purpose.

2Follow an email through its life

Keep the mailbox page on Activity Feed, and log in to one helper mailbox in another tab. Over minutes to hours you'll see:

EventWhat happened
SentAn email went from your mailbox to a helper.
FiledThe helper tidied it into its WarmupHub folder, away from its real inbox.
RescuedIt had landed in spam, and the helper moved it back out — a great sign.
Opened / StarredThe helper read it, sometimes starring it.
RepliedThe helper wrote back; the reply lands in your mailbox's WarmupHub folder.
What to expect over the first daySeveral "sent", most "filed", some "opened", a few "replied". Opens and replies are deliberately delayed to look natural, so check back later rather than waiting on the screen. The Sent Emails and Logs tabs show more detail, and each helper's own page shows the other side of the conversation.
PART 10

Run an inbox placement test

This answers the big question: where does my mail actually land? It sends a test email to a set of "judge" mailboxes and reports whether it reached the inbox or spam. It needs at least three working judges.

1Add judge mailboxes one-time, slightly technical

Judges are added with a short command rather than a web page. If this part is uncomfortable, ask whoever set up the app to run it for you, giving them three spare mailbox addresses and their app passwords. (The command uses a private key from the project's settings; it should never be shared publicly.)

What to expectEach judge is checked to confirm it can be logged in to. Any judge that can't log in is set aside automatically and not used.

2Run the test

Open your mailbox page, go to the Placement tab, and click Run Placement Test.

What to expectThe test starts as "queued", becomes "running", and finishes in a few minutes. The result shows the share of judges where your email reached the Primary inbox, the Promotions tab, or Spam, plus any that didn't arrive. For a new address, a high spam share is normal — it should shrink as warming continues.
If it says "too few seed mailboxes"You have fewer than three working judges. Add more, or fix the one that failed its check, then run the test again. (The admin test account can run unlimited tests.)
PART 11

Score, graduation & advice

1Read the score

The score (0–100) is worked out once a day from three things: your DNS health, your blocklist status, and your placement results. The arrow beside it shows whether it's rising, falling or steady. It only counts days where real measurements were available, so the first day or two can look low — that's expected.

2Understand "graduation"

A mailbox graduates when all of these are true: it has been warming long enough (about 3 weeks on the fast speed, 5 weeks on normal, 8 weeks on slow); it has seven days of measured scores averaging 80 or more; and a recent placement test shows spam at 5% or less. When it's otherwise ready and has no recent test, the app runs one free test by itself.

On graduation, the mailbox is marked graduated, it leaves the pool, and you get an email plus a short readiness report with a recommended daily sending amount. You can't skip the waiting time — building real trust is the whole point.

3Ask for an AI explanation needs a setup key

The mailbox's diagnostics page can ask an AI to explain, in plain words, what's holding the mailbox back and how to fix it, in order. This needs an AI key to be added to the app's settings (it's a placeholder on a fresh install). Without it, you still get a clear list of detected problems and fix steps — just not the AI summary.

PART 12

Everyday use: pause, resume, remove

1Pause and resume

On a mailbox's page, Pause stops all new emails and clears any that were waiting. Resume starts it again. Pausing is always safe, and warm-up progress is kept.

Sometimes the app pauses a mailbox for you, and the page says why:

Reason shownWhat it meansWhat to do
Bounce rateToo many emails to partners were rejected.Check your helper addresses are real and active, then resume.
BlocklistThe address appeared on a "bad sender" list.Follow the diagnostics steps to get removed; it resumes after a clean check.
Access revokedThe app password was cancelled or changed.Create a new app password and use the reconnect button.

2The shared pool, and removing a mailbox

On the mailbox page, Pool participation lets this mailbox also trade emails with other companies' mailboxes. On your own setup there are no other companies, so you'll see only the status change. To stop entirely, use the disconnect option — it erases the saved password and stops all activity. The Account page can export all your data or delete the account; only delete when you're finished.

A note on the preview screensSome pages show sample data and a dashed "Preview" banner — Analytics, Deliverability, Placement tests, Sending domains, and most Settings pages. These are design drafts, not yet connected, so don't judge the real product by them. Everything on Accounts, Warming pool, and each mailbox's own page is real and live.
